Out of every 1,000 uranium atoms dug out of the ground, about seven are uranium-235. Nearly all the rest are uranium-238, according to the US Nuclear Regulatory Commission (NRC). Uranium enrichment is the industrial job of raising those seven to somewhere between 30 and 50.

The word now turns up in diplomatic news as often as in engineering. In November 2025 a joint US-South Korea fact sheet included a line on Korean civil enrichment, and formal follow-up talks opened in Seoul in June 2026. Most coverage, though, never explains what the numbers in the headlines separate.

This piece walks through the enrichment scale, the physics and economics behind it, who supplies the world's enrichment, and what the US-Korea 123 agreement actually says about enrichment.

## 1. What uranium enrichment is

Light-water reactors, the type that makes up most of Korea's fleet and most of the world's, run on fuel with uranium-235 raised to 3% to 5% (NRC; World Nuclear Association). Natural uranium's 0.7% is too lean for them. Enrichment is not chemistry. It physically sorts two isotopes whose masses differ only slightly.

Today's commercial workhorse is the gas centrifuge. In the NRC's description, uranium is converted to uranium hexafluoride (UF6) gas and spun at high speed in a cylinder, and the slightly heavier molecules carrying uranium-238 drift outward. The World Nuclear Association (WNA) puts rotor speeds at 50,000 to 70,000 rpm, with the outer wall moving at 400 to 500 meters per second.

One machine shifts the ratio only a little. So plants link many of them into **cascades** that step the concentration up stage by stage.

### SWU: how the industry measures the work

Enrichment effort is measured in separative work units, or SWU. Plant capacity is quoted in SWU per year, and utilities buy enrichment as SWU.

WNA offers a worked example. "Tails" here means the depleted uranium left behind after the uranium-235 has been stripped out.

- **With 0.25% tails**: one kilogram of 5% fuel takes about 10.4 kg of natural uranium and 7.9 SWU.
- **With 0.20% tails**: the natural uranium falls to 9.4 kg, but the work rises to 8.9 SWU.

Feed and work trade off against each other. The work is also front-loaded: the bulk of it goes into handling large volumes of low-concentration material. WNA points to the relatively small increment of effort needed to go beyond normal reactor levels as the reason enrichment is treated as a sensitive technology.

## 2. Uranium enrichment levels: LEU vs HALEU vs HEU

The scale breaks down like this:

- **Natural uranium**: about 0.7% uranium-235 and 99.3% uranium-238 (NRC).
- **Low-enriched uranium**: LEU, 3% to 5% for large light-water reactors. The US Department of Energy (DOE) describes existing reactor fuel as no more than 5%, and WNA notes that material up to 10% is sometimes called LEU+.
- **High-assay low-enriched uranium**: HALEU, above 5% and below 20% in DOE's definition. DOE says most advanced reactor designs need it to shrink their cores and run longer between refuelings.
- **Highly enriched uranium**: HEU, 20% and above (NRC glossary).
- **Weapons use**: WNA notes that weapons material would have to be enriched to at least 90% in plants specially designed for it.

Despite the "high-assay" label, HALEU is legally the top slice of low-enriched uranium. As more [non-light-water SMR designs that need HALEU](/en/posts/smr-global-status-2026/) move toward licensing, supply in this band becomes a bottleneck. DOE's HALEU page still says the material is not currently available from domestic suppliers (checked November 3, 2026).

### Why 20% is the line

Twenty percent is not a cliff set by physics. According to a 2016 US National Academies report, the boundary comes from a definition the US Atomic Energy Commission introduced in 1954, and the United States chose 20% as the fuel enrichment for the research reactors it offered abroad at the 1955 Geneva conference on the peaceful uses of atomic energy.

The same report notes that critical mass drops sharply as enrichment rises, with no sharp break anywhere along the curve. What detailed analyses found instead is that around 20% minimizes the weapons attractiveness of both the uranium route and the plutonium route. A 2016 paper by Andrew Brown and Alexander Glaser in Science & Global Security notes that the International Atomic Energy Agency (IAEA) uses the same 20% boundary, and that its origins are surprisingly obscure.

## 3. A number for use and control, not a danger score

Having studied nuclear engineering and radiation science, I find one misreading comes up more than any other. People treat the enrichment percentage as a hazard rating, as if 5% fuel were five times as dangerous as 1% fuel.

The number really tells you two things. It tells you which kind of reactor the material can run, and how tightly the international system needs to track it. Most of the radioactivity inside an operating reactor comes from the fission products created as it runs, not from the starting enrichment. So the 20% line reads less like "danger starts here" and more like "the next tier of controls starts here."

That is also why a power reactor cannot become a bomb. WNA states that a commercial power reactor cannot under any circumstances explode like a nuclear bomb, because its fuel is not enriched beyond about 5%. A nuclear explosion needs a chain reaction that runs away in the tiny interval before highly enriched material blows itself apart. As our [criticality explainer](/en/posts/reactor-criticality-explained/) shows, a reactor's supercritical state is only a sliver above 1, and delayed neutrons set the pace.

None of this means reactors cannot have explosions. WNA describes Chernobyl's as a steam explosion followed by a second explosion that was probably hydrogen. They were not nuclear explosions, but accidents can take other paths.

## 4. Who enriches the world's uranium

### Four suppliers, one dominant

WNA's table, drawn from the association's Nuclear Fuel Report 2025, puts world enrichment capacity at about 68 million SWU a year in 2025. Shares below are my own calculation from that table.

| Supplier | 2025 capacity (thousand SWU/yr) | Share |
|---|---|---|
| Rosatom (Russia) | 29,133 | about 43% |
| Urenco (Europe) | 17,300 | about 25% |
| CNNC (China) | 13,789 | about 20% |
| Orano (France) | 7,500 | about 11% |

The CNNC figure is a WNA estimate that assumes China builds enough capacity for its own fleet. Even so, more than two-fifths of global capacity sits in Russia.

Governments are trying to change that. The United States enacted the Prohibiting Russian Uranium Imports Act in May 2024 and banned imports of Russian low-enriched uranium from August 11, 2024 (World Nuclear News). The Energy Secretary can grant waivers only until January 1, 2028, and the ban runs to the end of 2040. WNA reports that the United Kingdom also banned Russian uranium imports from May 2026.

### Where South Korea's reactor fuel is enriched

South Korea has always run an open fuel cycle, with no enrichment and no reprocessing (WNA). WNA's South Korea profile, updated November 2, 2026, lays out the chain:

- **Uranium**: about 4,700 tonnes a year, from Kazakhstan, Canada, Australia, Niger and elsewhere.
- **Enrichment**: Russia's Tenex, Urenco, Centrus of the United States and Orano of France have supplied enrichment services.
- **Fabrication**: KEPCO Nuclear Fuel (KEPCO NF), Korea's fuel fabricator, has capacity for 700 tonnes a year of PWR fuel and 400 tonnes of CANDU fuel, and supplies all the needs of Korea Hydro & Nuclear Power (KHNP, the state company that runs every Korean reactor).

KHNP has been widening that supplier list. It signed a long-term enrichment contract with Areva NC, now Orano, in 2007 and took a 2.5% stake in Orano's Georges Besse II enrichment plant in mid-2009. In February 2025 it signed a 10-year enriched uranium contract with Centrus, to be supplied from new capacity in Piketon, Ohio, and contingent on Centrus securing financing (WNN). An April 2026 memorandum with Orano was followed on September 8, 2026 (local time), by a letter of intent on long-term enrichment cooperation signed at the Élysée Palace in Paris (WNN; Korean daily Kyeongbuk Maeil).

I could not find a primary KHNP or KEPCO Nuclear Fuel document giving supply shares by country. So this piece does not state what percentage of Korea's enrichment is Russian.

## 5. The view from Korea: the 123 agreement and enrichment

### What the 2015 agreement says

The current US-ROK agreement for peaceful nuclear cooperation, usually called the 123 agreement, was signed on June 15, 2015 and entered into force on November 25, 2015 (National Archives of Korea). According to the Congressional Research Service (CRS), it replaced the 1974 agreement and runs for 20 years with an automatic five-year renewal.

On the fuel cycle, three provisions matter:

- **Enrichment below 20 percent**: possible in South Korea only after consultation through a new high-level bilateral commission and further written agreement by the United States (CRS). WNA puts it more bluntly: the agreement contains no provision allowing Korea itself to enrich, only a path through consultation.
- **Reprocessing**: US consent is still needed to reprocess US-origin spent fuel. Korea may run only the first stage of pyroprocessing, which separates no plutonium (CRS). Our piece on [reprocessing versus direct disposal](/en/posts/reprocessing-vs-disposal/) covers what that constraint means for Korea's spent fuel.
- **Consent rights**: both sides hold reciprocal consent rights over retransfers and over enrichment or reprocessing of covered material (CRS).

So the short answer to "why can't South Korea enrich uranium?" is that it can only do so with a written US arrangement that does not yet exist.

### What has happened since October 2025

- **November 13, 2025 (US time), joint fact sheet**: the United States said that, consistent with the 123 agreement and subject to US legal requirements, it supports the process that will lead to the ROK's civil uranium enrichment and spent fuel reprocessing for peaceful uses (WNN). The object of support is a process.
- **November 14, 2025, Seoul briefing**: National Security Adviser Wi Sung-lac said enrichment and reprocessing, not nuclear-powered submarines, were the issues debated until the very end, and that turning support into actual rights would require follow-up talks to adjust the existing agreement, according to Korean daily Kyunghyang Shinmun.
- **June 2-3, 2026, first round**: Korea's First Vice Foreign Minister Park Yoon-joo and US Under Secretary of State for Political Affairs Allison Hooker opened the talks in Seoul. Day two focused on enrichment and reprocessing, and a second round in Washington was floated for as early as July (Korean news agency Newsis).
- **August 5, 2026, ministry report**: the July round had slipped with no new date, Korean broadcaster MTN reported. Foreign Minister Cho Hyun said the ministry would speed up talks on submarine requirements and build the conditions for enrichment and reprocessing.
- **Late October 2026 reports**: Korean daily Hankyung reported on October 27 that no date had been set for a second high-level round since the June talks. Seoul Economic Daily reported on October 29 that a US official meeting reporters at the US Embassy in Seoul saw potential for cooperation, but said further discussion related to the 123 agreement was needed and challenges remained.
- **November 1, 2026, column**: a Washington correspondent's column in Seoul Economic Daily, marking a year since the fact sheet, judged that the security agreements had made no clear progress since June.

As of November 3, 2026, no amended agreement and no written enrichment arrangement has been announced. According to the late-October reports, the second high-level round has not yet been held. Whether the issue is handled inside the current framework or by amending it, and where and in what form any Korean enrichment would happen, all remain open. This track also runs separately from the [US-Korea reactor investment package](/en/posts/us-korea-nuclear-package/).

Reading this from Korea, I watch the gap between "supports" and "permits." The fact sheet promised a process, not a right. As of early November 2026, the formal talks have met once.

## Where this goes next

When an enrichment number shows up in the news, place it on the scale first. At or below 5% is today's reactor fuel, below 20% is advanced reactor fuel, and 20% and above is where controls step up.

Two things are worth watching. On the diplomatic side, the test is a written arrangement or an amended 123 agreement, not another statement of support. On the supply side, it is how US and UK bans on Russian uranium, and new Western capacity, show up in Korean fuel contracts.
