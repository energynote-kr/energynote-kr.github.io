On November 30, 2016, a boiling water reactor in northern Taiwan was barred from operating for a reason that had nothing to do with its reactor. Kuosheng unit 1 had practically no empty cells left in its spent fuel pool, according to a paper later presented by an engineer from the operator, Taipower. It did not run again until June 2017.

That is the clearest answer to a question that comes up whenever a country's spent fuel pools approach saturation: what happens when a spent fuel pool is full? The plant does not switch off the day the last rack fills. It stops at the next refueling, because the fuel that has to come out has nowhere to go.

This explainer walks through the physics behind that, what "full" really means for spent fuel pool capacity, and the plants in Taiwan, South Korea and the United States where storage space, not safety or economics, set the schedule.

## 1. Why used fuel goes into water at all

A reactor that has been shut down keeps producing heat. Fission leaves behind radioactive fragments, and their decay goes on whether the chain reaction does or not. The World Nuclear Association puts this decay heat at about 6.5% of full power at the moment of shutdown, about 1.5% after an hour, 0.4% after a day and 0.2% after a week.

Those percentages sound small. They are not. A 2006 report by the US National Research Council on spent fuel storage gives an example plant whose pool held 3.9 megawatts of decay heat ten days after a one-third-core offload. That is roughly the output of two thousand electric kettles, running day and night.

Water does two jobs at once. It carries the heat away to cooling systems, and it shields workers from radiation.

In the National Research Council's example plant, about 8 meters (26 feet) of water sits above the top of the racks, enough to provide substantial shielding even while an assembly is being moved. It is also why spent fuel glows blue, an effect we explained in [our piece on Cherenkov radiation](/en/posts/cherenkov-radiation/).

After some years the heat falls enough for air to do the cooling. The World Nuclear Association says used fuel is normally kept under water for at least five years before it goes to dry storage, while noting that some canister designs accept fuel cooled for only three. The US Nuclear Regulatory Commission (NRC) simply says that after a few years the fuel can be removed. There is no single universal number.

## 2. What "full" means: the full core reserve

It helps to picture a pool's positions in three groups:

- **Fuel already stored**: everything discharged over the plant's life so far.
- **The next discharge**: room for the assemblies that will come out at the next refueling outage.
- **The full core reserve**: enough empty space to unload the entire core at once if the reactor vessel or its internals need inspection or repair.

The National Research Council report describes keeping room for a full core as standard industry practice, and notes that US regulations do not require it. In operational terms, then, a pool is effectively full not when the last rack position is taken, but when the plant can no longer protect that reserve and still discharge its next batch. Utilities do not always publish how their own "saturation" figures treat the reserve, and we could not confirm how Korea's published fill rates handle it.

Pools are concrete structures that cannot be enlarged, as the NRC notes. That leaves three ways to buy space:

- **Re-racking**: replacing storage racks with denser ones that put assemblies closer together. US operators began doing this in the late 1970s, raising capacity in some pools by up to about a factor of five (National Research Council).
- **Moving fuel between units**: shifting older assemblies to a sister unit's pool that still has room. Korea's Hanbit 1 has been doing exactly this, as [our report on Korea's spent fuel](/en/posts/korea-spent-fuel-dry-storage/) describes.
- **Dry storage**: loading cooled fuel into sealed metal or concrete casks, which frees pool positions for fresh discharges.

## 3. Why a full pool stops a reactor at refueling

Light-water reactors stop every 12, 18 or 24 months and replace a quarter to a third of their fuel assemblies, according to the World Nuclear Association. The spent assemblies have to go to the pool. If there is no room, fresh fuel cannot go in.

Leaving the old fuel where it is does not work either. A core made of depleted fuel lacks the reactivity to stay [critical](/en/posts/reactor-criticality-explained/) for another full cycle. So the reactor can finish the cycle it is on, but it cannot restart after the outage.

There is one way to stretch things. An operator can cut power to make the remaining reactivity last longer, which is what Taipower did at Kuosheng in 2021. That buys months, not years.

Heavy-water CANDU reactors, the type Korea runs at Wolsong, are different. They refuel on line, swapping a few bundles every day. With no outage cycle to wait for, a CANDU plant whose storage is full has very little slack at all.

## 4. Plants that actually ran out of room

### Taiwan: Kuosheng and Chinshan

Kuosheng 1 had already been through two re-racking projects that took its pool to 4,398 positions when operation was prohibited from November 30, 2016, according to a 2018 Pacific Basin Nuclear Conference paper by a Taipower engineer. The fix was to install four racks in the cask loading pool next to it, and the unit came back on line in June 2017.

Five years later the pool was full again. Taipower said Kuosheng 1 would have had to shut in March 2021 had it not lowered power to 80%, and it closed the unit for good about six months before its licence was due to expire on December 27, 2021 (World Nuclear News, July 1, 2021). A dry storage facility for the plant had been delayed by a dispute with the New Taipei City government, which opposed a permanent used fuel facility within its jurisdiction.

At the neighboring Chinshan plant the same bottleneck blocked decommissioning instead. Its dry storage facility was built in 2013, but local permits were withheld for years, so fuel stayed in the reactor after unit 1's licence expired in December 2018. Taiwan's Nuclear Safety Commission says the facility received its operating licence on May 1, 2025, and that, as of July 2026, the cores of both units had been fully defueled.

### South Korea: Wolsong, 2019 to 2022

Korea came close in a different way.

In the second quarter of 2019, the dry storage at Wolsong, Korea's CANDU site, was more than 96% full and projected to saturate in November 2021, Korean outlet Electronic Times reported. Korea Hydro & Nuclear Power (KHNP), the state utility that operates all Korean reactors, expected new MACSTOR storage modules to take at least 19 months to build. The paper reported that, without a start by April 2020, Wolsong 2 to 4 could have to stop.

Not everyone accepted the deadline. A government committee then re-examining Korea's spent fuel policy argued that saturation would come about four months later. Korean daily Money Today reported in February 2020 that this would put saturation in March 2022 and push the last start date to August. In the end construction began in August 2020 and the new modules were approved for use in March 2022, so the units kept running.

### The United States: Prairie Island

In Minnesota the constraint was political. After a 1993 court ruling that dry casks at the Prairie Island plant needed legislative approval, a 1994 state law allowed 17 casks, according to the Minnesota Legislative Reference Library. The library notes that 17 casks were not enough for the waste the plant would generate, and the legislature acted again in a 2003 special session. The broader US picture was similar: in 2000 the industry projected that, without interim storage, roughly three or four plants a year would run out of pool space (National Research Council).

## 5. Korea's clock

As of September 2026, Korea's pools were projected to saturate at Hanbit in 2030, Hanul in 2031 and Kori in 2032, or 2028 at Kori without added dense racks. Reports on the draft third basic plan for high-level waste, published on November 3, 2026, gave the same years and put fill rates at the end of the third quarter at 93.4% for Kori, 86.0% for Hanbit and 76.1% for Hanul (SBS Biz and Money Today Broadcasting).

According to the same reports, the draft aims to confirm a repository site by 2038 after a call for municipal applicants in 2027, with interim storage operating before 2050 and disposal before 2060. The draft is open for public comment from November 4 to 23 (Kyongbuk Maeil, November 4), with a final plan due by the end of 2026 (Money Today Broadcasting). Site-by-site figures and the licensing calendar are in our [September report on Korea's spent fuel](/en/posts/korea-spent-fuel-dry-storage/).

Until then, the bridge is dry storage inside the plant fence. KHNP and the government describe it as the minimum needed to keep reactors running. Host communities argue that a "temporary" facility without a national destination becomes permanent by default.

Both positions rest on something real, and the Taiwanese record shows what happens when that dispute is not settled before the pools fill.

One distinction matters when reading Korean news. Wolsong 2 cannot restart because its operating licence expired on November 1, 2026, not because of storage; see [our tracker of Korea's idle reactors](/en/posts/idle-reactors-license-bottleneck/).

## Where this goes next

Having studied nuclear engineering, I read a saturation year as a deadline on an outage schedule rather than an off switch. The useful question is not "when is the pool full" but "does dry storage open before the first outage that cannot discharge its fuel".

For Hanbit that means comparing a July 2030 dry storage start with a 2030 saturation year. Taiwan learned what a gap between those two dates costs. Korea still has a few years to avoid learning it the same way.
