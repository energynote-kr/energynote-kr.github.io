On October 27, 2026, Korea's Nuclear Safety and Security Commission (NSSC), the country's nuclear regulator, voted 5 to 2 to let Kori 3 and 4 run for another ten years. Seven of its nine members took part (Kyunghyang Shinmun; Cheonji Ilbo, October 27, 2026). The two 950 MW units near Busan had been idle since their licenses expired.

Approval does not mean power. Korea Hydro & Nuclear Power (KHNP), the state utility that operates every Korean reactor, aims to restart them one after the other in the first half of 2027, after equipment upgrades and inspections. This post first appeared on October 8 as a look at why four reactors were stuck; it has been updated as of October 29 with the outcome and the units still waiting.

> **Key takeaways**
> - Kori 3 and 4 were approved on October 27 to operate until September 28, 2034 and August 6, 2035. KHNP targets restart in the first half of 2027.
> - The accident-management plans that were deferred twice in September were approved on October 15 for Kori 3 and 4 and Hanbit 1 and 2. A decision on Hanbit 1 and 2 continued operation is targeted for the first half of 2027.
> - The ten years run from the original design-life expiry, not from approval, so Korean media put the real extra operation at about seven years for Kori 3 and eight for Kori 4. Wolsong 2's license ends on November 1, and as of an October 21 report KHNP had not yet filed its application.

## 1. What was decided on October 27

Korean reactors carry a design-life date in their license, 40 years for these pressurized water reactors. When it arrives the unit must stop until the NSSC approves continued operation, backed by a technical review from KINS (Korea Institute of Nuclear Safety, the NSSC's review body). How that process compares with the US, Japan and France is covered in [our process explainer](/en/posts/korea-reactor-license-renewal/).

The table is as of October 29, 2026. Targets are windows the NSSC or KHNP reportedly gave, not fixed dates.

| Unit | License expired | Status on Oct. 29 | Next step |
|---|---|---|---|
| Kori 3 | Sept. 28, 2024 | Approved Oct. 27; runs to Sept. 28, 2034 | Restart target H1 2027 |
| Kori 4 | Aug. 6, 2025 | Approved Oct. 27; runs to Aug. 6, 2035 | Restart target H1 2027 |
| Hanbit 1 | Dec. 2025 | Plan approved Oct. 15; under review | Decision target H1 2027 |
| Hanbit 2 | Sept. 11, 2026 | Same stage as Hanbit 1 | Decision target H1 2027 |
| Wolsong 2 | Nov. 1, 2026 | Application not yet filed (Oct. 21 report) | KHNP aims for review in H2 2027 |

### The vote and the dissent

One member was absent and another left in the afternoon, so seven voted. Five, including Chairman Choi Won-ho, were in favor; commissioners Park Jong-woon and Jin Jae-yong opposed (Kyunghyang Shinmun).

Jin, according to Kyunghyang, said the way accident scenarios were set up, including what he saw as underestimation he had raised since the accident-management plan stage, needed more review, and that he would vote no if a vote was held that day. The central dispute is a simultaneous accident at several units on one site, as at Fukushima in 2011.

### The basis for approval

- **Review length**: KHNP submitted its periodic safety review in September 2022 and applied for the license change in November 2023. KINS reviewed from September 2022 to September 2026, about four years, and the Nuclear Safety Expert Committee checked the results over six months from March 2026 (Financial News, October 27, 2026).
- **Safety margin**: the NSSC judged, from component life assessments and planned replacements and upgrades, that margins are sufficient for the extended period.
- **Radiation impact**: reported maximum public dose was 0.096 mSv in normal operation against a 0.25 mSv criterion, and 103.3 mSv for major accidents including severe accidents, against 250 mSv (Financial News; Global Economic News).
- **Before restart**: the NSSC must confirm the upgrades through on-site inspection. Choi said the commission would verify them thoroughly.

This is Korea's fourth continued-operation approval, after Kori 1, Wolsong 1 and Kori 2 (Kyunghyang Shinmun). On October 27 itself, 8 of Korea's 26 reactors (7,150 MW) were off line for maintenance or license expiry, by one outlet's count (Callnews24), which [the fleet map](/en/posts/korea-reactor-fleet-map/)'s "26 operating" label does not show.

## 2. The accident-management plan: a gate cleared at the third try

An **accident-management plan** is the comprehensive manual for limiting damage in a severe accident, covering scope, equipment, strategies and training (Asia Economy Daily, September 30, 2026). Legally it is separate from the continued-operation license. In practice it came first, as it did for Kori 2.

### From deferral to approval

- **September 11** (meeting 2026-14): the plans for all four units were listed for the first time and carried over.
- **September 30** (meeting 2026-15): some commissioners questioned the drafting standards, a dispute over whether to vote followed, and the item was sent for re-listing (Asia Economy Daily; Kyunghyang Shinmun).
- **October 15** (meeting 2026-16): the plans for Kori 3 and 4 and Hanbit 1 and 2, with related equipment license changes, passed 7 to 2, Park and Jin opposing. Kori 3 and 4 continued operation was tabled the same day for the first time and held over (Kyunghyang Shinmun, October 15, 2026).
- **October 27** (meeting 2026-17): continued operation approved, 12 days after the plan.

For Kori 2, the plan passed on October 23, 2025 and continued operation on November 13 (Segye Ilbo). Same order, slightly shorter gap this time.

### Where the technical review and the commissioners parted

KINS confirmed after site inspections that accident-management capability meets the standard (Special Economy, September 12, 2026), and the expert committee found the review sound (Asia Economy Daily). The dissent was about how accident scenarios are framed.

The Korea Federation for Environmental Movements argued that a multi-unit probabilistic safety assessment study was not reflected in the review, and that the NSSC approved unit by unit without fully examining a site where reactors are clustered (Kyunghyang Shinmun, October 27, 2026). Long deliberation is not new: Saeul 3's operating license was first discussed on December 19, 2025 and granted on December 30 (Newsis), one step in [the seven-stage licensing path](/en/posts/korea-nuclear-licensing-process/).

## 3. Wolsong 2 on November 1, and the seven still waiting

Wolsong 2 (700 MW) was licensed in November 1996, and its 30-year license ends on November 1, 2026 (Maeil Shinmun, October 21, 2026). It is a CANDU heavy-water unit, so extension brings major work such as pressure-tube replacement; Maeil Shinmun reported that, because Canadian suppliers need years to deliver and install the parts, the real extended run would be only about six to seven years even with approval.

The filing is late. KHNP submitted periodic safety reviews for Wolsong 2 to 4 in April 2024 and finished public consultation in May 2025, but had not applied for the license change as of October 21 (Maeil Shinmun). A KHNP official told the paper the economic evaluation is under way and the company plans to file soon.

- **KHNP's reason**: a 2021 economic-evaluation guideline uses the Korea Power Exchange settlement price rather than retail tariffs, and KHNP says heavy-water units cost about 10 percent more to generate than light-water ones, which makes the case hard to show (Maeil Shinmun).
- **Already in an outage**: Wolsong 2 entered a planned maintenance outage on September 1, 2025 (Global Economic News, September 2, 2025). It has stayed down since, after some pipe supports were found to have been built differently from the design drawings (TBC, March 10, 2026; Callnews24, October 27, 2026). KHNP says the mismatch is not directly safety-related and will not affect the continued-operation process.
- **Next in line**: Wolsong 3's license ends in December 2027 and Wolsong 4's in February 2029. According to Jaekyung Ilbo (October 27, 2026), KHNP aims to bring Hanbit 1 and 2 and Hanul 1 and 2 to the NSSC in the first half of 2027, and Wolsong 2 to 4 in the second half.

Excluding Kori 3 and 4, seven units, about 5.9 GW, are waiting on continued-operation review: Hanbit 1 and 2, Hanul 1 and 2 and Wolsong 2 to 4 (NewDaily, October 28, 2026).

## 4. The cost argument and the ten-year clock

### Sales not made, fuel burned instead

- **Rep. Hwang Jung-a's office**: using KHNP data, it estimated the four units could have produced about 23.95 million MWh through September 22, 2026. At the 2025 average nuclear sales price of KRW 79 per kWh, that is KRW 1,891.9 billion (about $1.4 billion) in foregone sales (Maeil Shinmun, September 29, 2026). NewDaily reported that Hwang put it at about 25.3 million MWh and roughly KRW 2 trillion (about $1.5 billion) at the October 13 parliamentary audit.
- **Korea Economic Daily**: replacing Hanbit 2's output with LNG for a 10-month stop would cost about KRW 440 billion (about $326 million) more, assuming a fuel-price gap and an 80 percent capacity factor (September 11, 2026).

The two answer different questions, foregone revenue versus added fuel cost, so they cannot be added. In 2025 the KPX settlement price was about KRW 79 per kWh for nuclear and KRW 158 for LNG ([how the bill splits by fuel](/en/posts/electricity-bill-energy-mix/)).

### When does the ten years start?

Under current practice, the ten years start at the original design-life expiry, not at approval or restart (Financial News, October 27, 2026; Eroun, October 28, 2026). That is why Kori 3's new end date is exactly ten years after September 28, 2024. Financial News put actual operation at about seven years for Kori 3 and eight for Kori 4.

NewDaily ran its own numbers: with a restart at the end of June 2027, Kori 3 loses about 33 months and keeps 7 years 3 months; Kori 4 loses about 22 months and keeps 8 years 2 months (October 28, 2026). I could not locate the statutory provision that sets this counting rule, so I describe it only as reported.

### Change the clock, or review more carefully?

- **Change how the period is counted**: industry wants post-expiry review time excluded, or longer terms. NewDaily noted that the US renews in 20-year steps and that Japan allows a single extension of up to 20 years beyond 40, with some shutdown periods excluded. An expert quoted by Maeil Shinmun suggested counting from the new license's effective date, or amending the law to 20 years.
- **Why it took so long**: Choi said ten applications arriving at once strained review staff. Kyung Hee University professor Jeong Beom-jin argued that the previous government's phase-out policy delayed report preparation (NewDaily). KHNP had earlier said reviews normally take three to four years (Maeil Shinmun, September 29, 2026).
- **The review was not thorough enough**: the two dissenting commissioners wanted the accident scenarios examined further, and environmental groups say approval came without a multi-unit accident assessment. An anti-nuclear coalition leader said reviewing Hanbit 1 and 2 the same way would fail to protect residents (Kyunghyang Shinmun).

I studied nuclear engineering and radiation science, and I do not think these demands cancel out. Settling open questions like multi-unit assessment ahead of time would speed reviews, and whether review time should eat into operating time is a separate policy choice. What standard the commission will hold to is still not fully visible from outside.

## Where this goes next

Four things to watch, as of October 29, 2026. First, whether Kori 3 and 4 clear upgrades, inspections and the NSSC's on-site check in time for the first-half 2027 target; every month of delay comes off the ten years. Second, when KHNP files for Wolsong 2 after its license lapses on November 1.

Third, the Hanbit 1 and 2 decision targeted for the first half of 2027, where the multi-unit question may return. Fourth, whether the debate over the expiry-based clock turns into a legal amendment. Delays were already a theme in this year's parliamentary audits, including the October 13 science committee session, summarized in [our Korean audit roundup](/posts/national-audit-2026-nuclear-energy/) (in Korean).
