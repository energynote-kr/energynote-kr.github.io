On April 22, 2026, a steel basemat 37 meters across and weighing about 2.1 million pounds was lowered to the bottom of a 35-meter shaft at Darlington, Ontario (ANS Nuclear News, June 5, 2026). It is the foundation of the reactor building for the world's first BWRX-300 small modular reactor.

Seven months later, on November 10, Ontario Power Generation (OPG) used its third-quarter results to say that unit 1 is no longer expected to meet its October 2030 commercial operation target or its C$7.7 billion estimate. It gave no new date and no new number.

The G7's first SMR is late and over budget. Is that evidence that the SMR idea does not work, or the normal pattern for a first-of-a-kind (FOAK) reactor? This piece looks at what OPG disclosed, why first units slip, and what it means for the BWRX-300 projects that follow, including the view from South Korea.

> **Key takeaways**
> - On November 10, 2026, OPG said Darlington unit 1 is no longer expected to reach commercial operation by October 2030 or to stay within C$7.7 billion (C$6.1 billion for the unit plus C$1.6 billion of shared infrastructure). Revised figures should follow a review due to be substantially complete in mid-2027.
> - OPG attributes the slip to GE Vernova Hitachi's unfinished first-of-a-kind engineering. Incomplete design at construction start, first-run modules and heavy site-specific civil works in a below-grade shaft all concentrate risk on unit 1.
> - Vogtle, Flamanville 3 and Olkiluoto 3 all ran years late as first units. Whether OPG's estimate for unit 4, about 33% below unit 1, holds will matter at least as much as unit 1's final bill.

## 1. The Darlington SMR delay: what OPG said

OPG's release of November 10, 2026 says four things.

- **Schedule**: unit 1 is no longer expected to achieve the previously disclosed commercial operation target of October 2030.
- **Cost**: nor is it expected to be completed within the C$7.7 billion estimate, which includes systems and services common to all four planned units.
- **Cause**: GE Vernova Hitachi Nuclear Energy (GEVH) is still doing engineering on its first-of-a-kind SMR, which OPG says has affected construction execution and supply chain performance.
- **Next step**: a comprehensive cost and schedule assessment, expected to be substantially complete by mid-2027.

The review list starts with completion of the engineering scope, including construction deliverables, followed by design maturity, first-of-a-kind complexity, inflation and geopolitical risk, and recovery measures. According to The Globe and Mail, GEVH acknowledged that engineering and design maturity are important considerations, while pointing to multiple interconnected contributions across engineering, procurement and construction that OPG's update also recognized (as cited by World Nuclear News, November 11, 2026).

### The baseline

The Canadian Nuclear Safety Commission (CNSC) issued the licence to construct on April 4, 2025. On May 8, 2025, Ontario approved the final investment decision for unit 1. Unit 1 at C$6.1 billion plus C$1.6 billion of shared infrastructure gives the C$7.7 billion now being exceeded; the projected cost of all four units, including interest, escalation and contingency, is C$20.9 billion (World Nuclear News, May 2025). Units 2 to 4 still need approval, which ANS expects in 2028.

Power Engineering reported that OPG had spent C$2.86 billion through September 30, 2026, and that on November 10 OPG withdrew most requested approvals for the SMR facilities from its pending 2027–2031 rate application at the Ontario Energy Board. This is not the first slip. When OPG chose the BWRX-300 in December 2021, it said the unit could be completed as early as 2028 (World Nuclear News, December 2, 2021).

## 2. Why first units run late: design maturity and first-run modules

The engineering items that lead OPG's review list match what ANS reported in June. Darlington started construction before the design was complete; civil and mechanical design was finished by the end of 2025, while electrical and instrumentation and control (I&C) design was still in progress as of June 2026.

A nuclear site moves on issued-for-construction drawings. Nuclear-grade equipment can only be ordered once its specifications are frozen, because suppliers need them to produce the quality records and qualification evidence the regulator expects. Late design means late orders. Late orders mean idle crews. OPG's release names both construction execution and supply chain performance, which fits this chain.

The modules are new too. The reactor building is built from about 1,200 steel-plate composite modules, steel shells filled with concrete, over eight levels. Aecon Kiewit Nuclear Partners (AKNP), the construction joint venture, fabricated the basemat modules in several Ontario shops and assembled them on site (ANS, June 5, 2026). Module costs fall once the same piece has been built many times. On unit 1, the shops and the field are both learning.

ANS also reports that GEVH plans to carry the one-team integrated project delivery model it developed with OPG to other BWRX-300 projects. Whether that pays off shows up from unit 2 onward. Scale helps a little: ANS puts the peak Darlington workforce at about 2,000, against up to 6,000 at a large US light-water reactor build.

## 3. Why the reactor sits in a 35-meter shaft

The BWRX-300 is a roughly 300 MW boiling water reactor derived from GE Hitachi's ESBWR, a larger design already cleared by the US Nuclear Regulatory Commission. The US Department of Energy describes how its isolation condenser turns steam back into water, starting natural circulation and passive cooling, and holding reactor pressure and temperature for seven days without power or operator action (DOE, February 19, 2020).

Natural circulation is why the reactor vessel is tall. The building's depth has a different reason: NRC review documents describe the reactor building as placed below grade in a vertical shaft to mitigate external events such as aircraft crashes, flooding and earthquakes.

Having studied nuclear engineering and radiation science, I read this as a trade. The plant has fewer pumps and valves, but much of the first unit's risk moves underground. OPG has assembled a tunnel boring machine for the condenser cooling water tunnel, with tunnelling expected to start in the last quarter of 2026 (OPG, November 10, 2026). Excavation, ground conditions and tunnels differ at every site, and none of them can be moved into a factory.

## 4. How this compares with large first-of-a-kind reactors

Delays and cost growth on first units are not unique to SMRs.

- **Vogtle 3 and 4** (US, AP1000): due in 2016 and 2017, they entered commercial operation in July 2023 and April 2024. The cost ended up more than double the $14 billion expected when Georgia regulators approved the project in 2009 (Southern Company; Georgia public radio WUGA, April 2024).
- **Flamanville 3** (France, EPR): construction began in December 2007, and the unit was not connected to the grid until December 2024. The estimate went from €3.3 billion (2005 values) to €13.2 billion (2015 values, EDF, December 2022).
- **Olkiluoto 3** (Finland, EPR): construction began in 2005 for completion in 2009; regular power production started in April 2023 (TVO).

Korea's own export was no exception. The UAE's Barakah plant, four APR1400 units, was a $20.4 billion contract in 2009 that grew to about $24 billion by completion, as covered in [our Barakah five-year review](/en/posts/barakah-five-years/).

Darlington's plan already assumes learning. World Nuclear News, citing OPG, put unit 4 at C$4.1 billion, about 33% less than unit 1 (May 2025), and noted that the estimates for units 2 to 4 are less developed than unit 1's. Unit 1 also carries the C$1.6 billion of shared infrastructure, so its price overstates what a repeat unit should cost.

The counterargument deserves its full weight. In May 2024 IEEFA, a US research group critical of nuclear expansion, argued that SMRs remain too expensive, too slow and too risky. One example it cites is NuScale's UAMPS project in Idaho, the Carbon Free Power Project. Its estimate reached $9.3 billion in January 2023 for a plant already downsized to 462 MWe, as reported by The Salt Lake Tribune, before the project was cancelled in November 2023. The SMR promise was never that small is cheap; it was that repetition makes it cheaper. That has not been demonstrated yet.

## 5. The view from Korea: suppliers, builders and the i-SMR lesson

Are Korean firms in this supply chain? As far as I could verify, no Korean company has a named role at Darlington unit 1 (as of November 16, 2026). GEVH awarded the reactor pressure vessel to BWXT in January 2025, to be made at its Cambridge, Ontario plant, and ANS lists AtkinsRéalis as integration engineer.

- **Samsung C&T** (Korean construction and engineering group): announced a strategic alliance with GEVH on October 7, 2025 to deploy the BWRX-300 in markets outside North America, naming a potential five units in Sweden. In September 2026 it also signed a four-way MoU with GE Vernova, Hitachi and Poland's SGE for Central and Eastern Europe and the UK. Neither has disclosed commercial terms.
- **Doosan Enerbility** (Korea's reactor-vessel manufacturer): I found no BWRX-300 equipment contract. Its SMR work is with NuScale, TerraPower and X-energy, which [our map of Korean firms in Western SMRs](/en/posts/korean-builders-western-smrs/) sorts by contract type.

The bigger lesson is for Korea's own design. The i-SMR combines four 170 MWe modules into a 680 MWe plant; its developers applied for Standard Design Approval in February 2026 and target a first unit in 2035 ([our i-SMR status explainer](/en/posts/korean-smr-ismr-status/)). Getting design approval before any site licence matures the design before concrete, which addresses Darlington's first problem. It does not make the first factory or first site any less new.

Korea's reputation in nuclear construction rests on repetition: the same APR1400 design built again and again, at home for KHNP (Korea Hydro & Nuclear Power, the state utility that operates all Korean reactors) and abroad at Barakah. For an SMR that advantage only appears from unit 2. An honest i-SMR business plan has to say, in numbers, who pays for unit 1.

## Where this goes next

Power Engineering noted that Darlington is meant to demonstrate the BWRX-300's commercial deployment pathway, so the delay matters beyond Ontario.

- **TVA Clinch River** (US): the NRC issued a construction permit in September 2026, but TVA has not announced a construction date. World Nuclear News reported on September 30, 2026 that TVA is still negotiating cost sharing with industry partners, and that Interim President and CEO Mike Skaggs made it a priority to ensure the people of the Tennessee Valley do not shoulder the costs of first-of-a-kind technology. As with [Dow's Long Mott permit](/en/posts/x-energy-long-mott-construction-permit/), the permit is in hand but the money question is not settled.
- **OSGE** (Poland): in June 2026, Orlen Synthos Green Energy applied for a contract for difference covering 14 BWRX-300 units at three sites, assuming the first unit starts at Włocławek in 2032 (World Nuclear News, June 30, 2026).

On the facts so far, Darlington looks like a textbook first-of-a-kind problem: construction started before the design was finished, the modules are a first run, and the hardest work is underground. OPG also flagged it roughly four years before the target date, which is worth noting.

It is too early for a verdict. The size of the revised estimate in mid-2027, whether follow-on projects can reuse Darlington's detailed design, and whether units 2 to 4 come in near the C$4.1 billion projected for unit 4 will matter at least as much as unit 1's final bill.
