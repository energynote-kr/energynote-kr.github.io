On September 1, 2026, Seoul National University's Nuclear Policy Center and GS Energy, a private Korean power company, hosted a policy forum on building a "private-sector-led SMR business model." SK Innovation presented a Korea–US cooperation model built around TerraPower. POSCO Holdings said it wants small modular reactors to supply the 1.7 GW of electricity its hydrogen-based steelmaking will need.

GS Energy spoke first, and what it put on the table was a list of **four uncertainties: reactor type and technology, revenue structure, community acceptance, and the legal and regulatory framework**. Ten days later, on September 11, Korea's SMR Special Act enters into force.

A law, a regulator's roadmap, a candidate site and a queue of corporate sponsors are all appearing at once. The simpler question, asked from inside the Korean nuclear sector, is how far along Korea's own reactor actually is, and what the calendar says.

> **Key takeaways**
> - i-SMR (four 170 MWe integral pressurized-water modules, 680 MWe per plant) filed for Standard Design Approval on February 27, 2026. The review has only begun: the regulator's plan puts the main test results at June 2028, so the "approval by 2028" target has almost no slack.
> - The SMR Special Act (in force September 11, 2026), the regulator's 2026–2030 roadmap and the Gijang candidate site (chosen in June) are building the frame, with construction targeted for 2030 and commercial operation for 2035. The construction permit, a buyer for the power and the cost are still blank.
> - The leading SMRs abroad aim for first power around 2030, roughly five years ahead. What to watch: the approval date, first-of-a-kind cost, power purchase agreements, and spent fuel.

## 1. i-SMR in 30 seconds

Korea's SMR is the **i-SMR** (innovative small modular reactor). It is an integral pressurized-water reactor, the same basic technology as Korea's operating fleet, packaged as a **170 MWe module**; four modules make one **680 MWe** plant.

A dedicated program office, the i-SMR Technology Development Agency, was launched in 2023 and is spending **KRW 399.2 billion** (about $295 million) in combined government and private funding over 2023–2028. The stated goal is to obtain **Standard Design Approval by 2028**.

The institutions that appear below, for readers outside Korea:

- **NSSC** (Nuclear Safety and Security Commission) is Korea's nuclear regulator, the counterpart of the US NRC.
- **KINS** (Korea Institute of Nuclear Safety) is the technical body that carries out the safety reviews on which NSSC decides.
- **KHNP** (Korea Hydro & Nuclear Power) is the state utility that operates all of Korea's reactors and is the expected owner and operator of the first i-SMR plant.
- **Standard Design Approval (SDA)** is a site-independent approval of a reactor design intended to be built repeatedly. It is the same concept as the NRC's SDA: it does not by itself allow construction anywhere. A construction permit and an operating license for a specific site come later.

If you want the basics of what an SMR is and what it does and does not change, see [our Korean SMR explainer](/posts/smr-explained/) (in Korean).

## 2. Where the clock stands

### The design review has barely started

The program office filed the SDA application with NSSC on **February 27, 2026**. The statutory review period is 24 months, but the review plan that KINS reported on **August 27, 2026** shows how much work sits in front of that clock. The document check produced **13 supplement requests**; the supplemented documents are due in **June 2027**, and the main test results, including thermal-hydraulic validation tests, are due in **June 2028** (status as of August 2026).

KINS itself described the completion date as "fluid." Read plainly, approval is more likely to come in the second half of 2028 or later, which means the 2028 target has almost no margin. It has not slipped yet, but nothing in the schedule is there to absorb a delay.

### The law and the regulator: a framework still under construction

The **SMR Special Act** passed the National Assembly on February 12, 2026 and takes effect on September 11. Its core is promotional: a national SMR development plan, a promotion committee, and designated R&D zones. On the same day the Act passed, NSSC published its **SMR Regulatory Framework Roadmap** (2026–2030), which schedules SMR-specific technical standards and amendments to the Nuclear Safety Act for **2028**.

That means the design review and the writing of the rules it will be judged against are running in parallel. Korean environmental groups made exactly this point in August: licensing has started while the regulatory system is still being built, and they asked the regulator to set the safety standards first. It is a fair criticism of the sequence. Regulators abroad have also written SMR-specific rules while reviewing first applications, but that is context, not an answer to it.

### The site: chosen, but the procedure is not

KHNP's site selection committee confirmed **Gijang County in Busan**, on the southeastern coast, as the first candidate site on June 17, 2026, with a score of 87.11 against 84.56 for Gyeongju. Gijang already hosts the Kori nuclear plant. The plan from here is SDA in 2028, then public consultation and a construction permit, then **construction start in 2030 and commercial operation in 2035**.

## 3. Side by side with SMRs abroad

The same five reference projects appear in [our world map of SMR licensing in 2026](/en/posts/smr-global-status-2026/); here they are only the yardstick.

- **NuScale (US)**: its 77 MWe module received NRC Standard Design Approval in May 2025. The 6 GW program with TVA and ENTRA1 remained a non-binding agreement as of August 2026.
- **TerraPower (US)**: NRC construction permit in March 2026 and groundbreaking in Wyoming in April. Targets are fuel loading in 2030 and commercial operation in 2031.
- **GE Vernova–Hitachi BWRX-300 (Canada)**: construction at Darlington began in May 2025, the operating license application was filed in March 2026, and grid connection is targeted for late 2030.
- **Oklo (US)**: construction of the first Idaho unit started in September 2025, but under the US Department of Energy's pilot-program authorization pathway rather than NRC licensing; a formal NRC application has not yet been filed. The company targets operation in 2028.
- **Linglong One (China)**: construction began in 2021 with commercial operation targeted for the first half of 2026. As of early August, World Nuclear News still described it as in pre-commissioning tests, with no announcement of fuel loading or criticality, so completion is not officially confirmed.

The leading group abroad is aiming at **first power around 2030**; i-SMR is aiming at **2035**, about five years behind. Two qualifications matter.

Nuclear target dates generally slip, in every country, and i-SMR is following the NuScale-style route of securing a repeatable standard design approval before any site-specific license, which front-loads the paperwork. Korea's reactor is one of the credible candidates, neither ahead of the pack nor out of it.

## 4. What the private sector asked for on September 1

The forum is worth a closer look because it shows where Korean industry thinks the bottlenecks are; GS Energy's four uncertainties are listed above. SK Innovation's contribution was a Korea–US model in which it works with TerraPower, whose Natrium reactor is the one already under construction in Wyoming. POSCO Holdings' 1.7 GW figure is the power its hydrogen reduction steelmaking would need, which is about two and a half i-SMR plants.

The two concrete requests were to **allow long-term power purchase agreements** (PPAs) for SMR output and to **write SMRs into the 12th Basic Plan for Long-term Electricity Supply and Demand**, the government's rolling 15-year plan that determines which power plants get built. In Korea's market, where the state utility KEPCO is the near-exclusive buyer of electricity, neither is a given. A reactor with no buyer for its power does not get built, and what industry asked for on September 1 was, in effect, a buyer.

## 5. Four realistic things to watch

1. **Licensing pace.** If SDA slips past 2028, construction and operation slip with it. The signals come in order: document supplements in June 2027, then test results in June 2028.
2. **First-of-a-kind cost.** The first US SMR project (NuScale with UAMPS, a group of Utah municipal utilities) was canceled in 2023 after cost increases. No public estimate exists yet for the first i-SMR plant, and the cost of new nuclear build is an open question in Korea as much as anywhere.
3. **A buyer for the power.** The PPA and Basic Plan requests from September 1 are the tell. Watch whether the 12th Basic Plan, when it is drafted, includes SMR capacity.
4. **Spent fuel.** A smaller reactor does not make waste disappear, and some analyses find that certain SMR designs produce more waste per unit of electricity, not less; see [our Korean SMR explainer](/posts/smr-explained/) (in Korean). Gijang, home to the Kori plant, will not be able to avoid that discussion.

The field is wide, but i-SMR revenue for Korean companies only becomes real once a construction permit follows the SDA, and the earliest realistic point for that is the second half of 2028 or later.

**Where this goes next.** The next dated events are September 11 (the Act takes effect), the first meeting of the promotion committee it creates, the drafting of the 12th Basic Plan, and the June 2027 document deadline at KINS. None of them will tell you whether Korea's SMR succeeds. Together they will tell you whether the 2028–2030–2035 calendar is still intact, and that is the one thing worth tracking.
