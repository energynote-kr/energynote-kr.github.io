Ask market research firms how big the nuclear decommissioning market is and you get answers between about $7.5 billion and $18.5 billion a year. Same industry, same year. The spread is not sloppiness: the firms are measuring different things.

This post sorts out those numbers, then follows the money to where it actually comes from and who gets paid to spend it. The second half looks at South Korea, which approved its first commercial reactor dismantling in 2025 and woke up to headlines about a "500-trillion-won market."

> **Key takeaways**
> - Most published estimates put decommissioning services at roughly $7.5–10 billion in 2025, growing 4–7% a year. Higher figures come from wider definitions that fold in waste management or legacy-site cleanup.
> - The money is not new demand. It comes from funds that utilities built up from electricity bills while their plants ran: about $100 billion in US trust funds, for example. The market grows as those funds get spent.
> - Korea's piece is one approved project so far, Kori-1, costed at about KRW 1,071.3 billion ($790 million) and due to finish in 2037. It is also the track record Korean firms need before anyone abroad will hire them.

## 1. How big is the nuclear decommissioning market?

Here are four widely cited estimates. They are commercial reports, so treat them as rough markers, not measurements.

Published estimates of the global nuclear decommissioning market (as of October 2026):

| Source | Base-year size | Forecast | Growth |
|---|---|---|---|
| IMARC | $7.5 billion (2025) | $11.2 billion by 2034 | 4.4% a year |
| Market.us | $8.4 billion (2025) | $12.5 billion by 2035 | 4.0% a year |
| GMI | $9.9 billion (2025) | $19.4 billion by 2035 | 6.8% a year |
| Meticulous | $18.5 billion (2025) | $30.2 billion by 2036 | 4.6% a year |

GMI is Global Market Insights. Its report covers decommissioning services specifically.

### Why the estimates disagree

- **Scope.** Some reports count only the dismantling of power reactors. Others add research reactors, fuel-cycle plants, waste handling, or bundle "maintenance and decommissioning services" together. That explains most of the gap between $8 billion and $18 billion.
- **What gets counted as revenue.** A reactor dismantling runs for ten years or more. Reports differ on whether they book the contract value when it is awarded or spread it across the years of work.
- **Base year.** A 2024 figure and a 2025 figure are not directly comparable when the market grows several percent a year.

A rough sanity check helps. Take $8–10 billion a year and run it over four decades: you land in the $320–400 billion range. That is the order of magnitude behind Korea's "KRW 500 trillion" headline, about $350–370 billion depending on the exchange rate. In other words, the headline is a cumulative global figure, not an annual one.

## 2. Where the work is

The pipeline is real. According to the World Nuclear Association (March 2026), more than 220 power reactors have been retired worldwide, but only about 25 have been fully dismantled. Most of the market is that backlog.

Permanently shut-down power reactors, selected countries (IAEA PRIS, January 2026):

| Country | Shut-down reactors | What stands out |
|---|---|---|
| United States | 41 | About ten taken all the way to license termination; roughly $100 billion in decommissioning trust funds (NRC data, 2024) |
| United Kingdom | 36 | Mostly early gas-cooled Magnox units; government nuclear provisions of £110.1 billion at 31 March 2025, with work planned to run until 2137 |
| Germany | 33 | Phase-out completed on April 15, 2023; operators pay for dismantling, while €24.1 billion went to the state KENFO fund for waste storage in 2017 |
| South Korea | 2 | Kori-1 approved for dismantling in 2025; Wolsong-1 next in line |

The UK figure is large because its early reactors cost far more to take apart per unit of capacity than modern light-water reactors. That makes the UK one of the largest pools of decommissioning money. It is mostly run through one public body, the Nuclear Decommissioning Authority (NDA).

### What one reactor costs

A 2016 OECD Nuclear Energy Agency survey put the total for a US reactor at $544–821 million in 2013 dollars. Korea's Kori-1 estimate, about $790 million, sits inside that band. Smaller and older designs cost more per megawatt, which is why per-reactor averages can mislead.

## 3. Who pays, and who gets paid

### The money comes from reserves

Utilities set money aside while a plant runs. In the US the main system is an external trust fund collected through electricity rates, outside the utility's control. Germany, the UK and Korea use their own versions of the same idea.

So a growing decommissioning market mostly means consumers' past payments being spent. That is useful for anyone reading company forecasts. The total budget is largely set; the competition is over who wins the contracts.

### Two strategies

- **Immediate dismantling** (called DECON in the US). The plant is taken apart soon after shutdown, and the site can be reused within about a decade.
- **Deferred dismantling** (SAFSTOR). The plant is sealed and left for 40 to 60 years so radioactivity decays first. This lowers worker dose, but it carries the risk that rules and costs change in the meantime.

A third option, entombment, encases the remaining material on site. It is rarely chosen for power reactors.

### The US license-transfer model

The United States produced a business model the rest of the world watches. Instead of hiring a contractor, the utility hands the whole plant to a specialist, together with the license and the trust fund. The specialist profits if it finishes for less than the fund holds.

- **EnergySolutions** took over Zion 1 and 2 near Chicago in 2010. The NRC released the site in 2023.
- **NorthStar** led the consortium that bought Vermont Yankee, and by the end of 2025 about 99% of the site had been demolished.
- **Holtec** took ownership of Indian Point in New York in 2021 for prompt dismantling.

Holtec's Palisades plant in Michigan shows how quickly the story can turn. Bought in 2022 to be decommissioned, it is now being prepared to restart instead, on the back of new demand for low-carbon power. The restart is not done yet: fuel loading was paused in August 2026 after a fuel assembly tipped inside the reactor.

## 4. Decommissioning is radiation management, not demolition

Taking a reactor apart is closer to a radiation-protection operation than to construction run in reverse. The order of work is what makes the difference.

1. **Fuel removal.** Taking the spent fuel out removes most of the facility's radioactivity. What remains is activated material in structures that sat near the core for decades.
2. **Decontamination.** Surface contamination is stripped with chemicals, high-pressure water or dry-ice blasting.
3. **Cutting and dismantling.** Large components, the reactor vessel and steam generators above all, are cut up with remote equipment and robots. Some areas are not safe for people to work in for long.
4. **Waste handling and site restoration.** The material is sorted by radioactivity level and disposed of accordingly, and the site is returned to a reusable condition.

Having studied nuclear engineering and radiation science, I find step 1 is the one outsiders underestimate. Until the fuel is gone, almost nothing else can move.

## 5. Kori-1: the only reference project Korea has

Every Korean decommissioning claim will be tested against Kori-1, near Busan. It was Korea's first commercial nuclear plant, ran from 1978 for close to 40 years, and was permanently shut down in 2017. Progress in detail is in [our Kori-1 tracker](/en/posts/kori1-decommissioning/); the short version follows.

- **Owner.** KHNP (Korea Hydro & Nuclear Power) is the state utility that operates all of Korea's reactors and a subsidiary of the national utility KEPCO. It owns Kori-1, pays for the work and awards the contracts.
- **Approval.** The Nuclear Safety and Security Commission (NSSC), Korea's nuclear regulator, approved the final decommissioning plan in June 2025.
- **Work under way.** In November 2025 a consortium led by Doosan Enerbility, Korea's maker of reactor vessels and steam generators, with KEPCO KPS among its members, began dismantling in the non-controlled area. That is the part of the plant where radiation never exceeded regulatory limits.
- **The plan after that.** Spent fuel to on-site dry storage by around 2031, decontamination and dismantling of the radiological area by around 2035, and site restoration by 2037.
- **Cost.** About KRW 1,071.3 billion, roughly $790 million, on KHNP's estimate, paid from its decommissioning reserve.

The item to watch is the dry storage facility. Until the fuel leaves the pool, the reactor building cannot be taken apart, so how quickly that facility is secured is the biggest variable in the 2037 date.

## 6. Korea's "500 trillion won," read correctly

Within days of the 2025 approval, Korean headlines announced that a KRW 500 trillion decommissioning market had opened. Section 1 already shows what that figure is: a cumulative global forecast over several decades. Three more points are usually left out.

**It is not Korea's.** The domestic market today is one approved project. Korea operates 26 reactors and will eventually decommission all of them, but over decades, not years.

**It is paid in installments.** A contractor's revenue is recognized across a ten-year-plus project. The distance between "500 trillion" and any company's annual results is very large.

**Experience is the entry ticket.** Only a short list of countries, the United States and Germany among them, have taken many commercial reactors to completion. No Korean company has yet done it. Kori-1 is the reference that overseas bids will be judged on.

Every time decommissioning makes the Korean news, small shares tagged with the theme jump and fall back. The pattern repeats. The useful question is what a company actually does in the chain, which is what the industry note below sets out.

## Where this goes next

Globally, watch the US trust-fund model and whether more shut plants are restarted rather than dismantled, as Holtec is attempting at Palisades. In Korea, the dated events are all on the Kori-1 site: the contracts after the current non-controlled-area work, the licensing of the on-site dry storage that gates the 2031 fuel removal, and whether the 2035 and 2037 milestones hold.
