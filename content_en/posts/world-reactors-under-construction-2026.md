At 20:41 local time on October 15, 2026, Slovakia's Mochovce 4 sent its first electricity to the grid, World Nuclear News reported the next day. Work on the unit started in 1986. That is forty years from first construction to first power.

October was busy. China's Tianwan 7 was connected to the grid on October 8, and Bangladesh's Rooppur 1 reached first criticality on October 11. So how many power reactors is the world building right now, where are they, and what does that number actually tell you?

> **Key takeaways**
> - The World Nuclear Association (WNA) counts 79 reactors under construction, 86,560 MWe gross, in its country table updated October 16, 2026. China has 38 of them.
> - Almost all are large pressurized water reactors, and two families dominate: China's Hualong One and Russia's VVER.
> - "Under construction" is a start-line statistic counted from first concrete. It does not tell you when reactors will run, and it includes projects that are stopped.

## 1. Reactors under construction in 2026: 79 units in 16 countries

The WNA's country table, updated October 16, lists 79 reactors under construction with a combined 86,560 MWe gross. Sixteen countries have at least one. The same table counts 443 operable reactors.

Even within one organization there is more than one number. The WNA's explainer "Plans For New Reactors Worldwide" (updated October 23) says about 80 in its text, while its unit-by-unit list below holds 75. Section 3 explains why.

The top eight countries (unit counts from the WNA country table of October 16, 2026; designs from its unit list updated October 23):

| Country | Units | Main designs | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|
| China | 38 | Hualong One, CAP1000, VVER-1200 | Includes fast and small reactors |
| India | 8 | VVER-1000, PHWR-700 | Includes the PFBR fast breeder |
| Russia | 7 | VVER-1200, VVER-TOI | Includes the BREST-OD-300 fast reactor |
| Egypt | 4 | VVER-1200 | El Dabaa 1–4 |
| South Korea | 4 | APR1400 | Saeul 3–4, Shin Hanul 3–4 |
| Türkiye | 4 | VVER-1200 | Akkuyu 1–4 |
| Bangladesh | 2 | VVER-1200 | Rooppur 1–2 |
| UK | 2 | EPR | Hinkley Point C 1–2 |

The other eight countries:

- **Two units each**: Japan (the ABWRs Ohma 1 and Shimane 3) and Ukraine (Khmelnitski 3 and 4). The WNA classifies all four as suspended.
- **One unit each**: Argentina (CAREM, suspended), Brazil (Angra 3, suspended), Canada (a BWRX-300 at Darlington), Hungary (Paks II unit 1), Iran (Bushehr 2) and Pakistan (Chashma 5).

China's 38 units almost match the other fifteen countries combined, which add up to 41. Construction there is spread across several state-owned groups, including CNNC, CGN and SPIC, building at many sites at once. Whatever happens in China moves the global total.

## 2. Which designs are being built

Counting the design names in the WNA list gives roughly this picture. Suspended units are included, and the totals can shift by a unit or two as the list is updated.

- **VVER family** (Russian design: VVER-1000, VVER-1200, VVER-TOI): around 25 units, in Russia, Türkiye, Egypt, Bangladesh, Hungary, Iran and India, suspended units in Ukraine, plus China's Tianwan and Xudabao sites.
- **Hualong One** (Chinese design): around 20 units, in China and at Chashma 5 in Pakistan.
- **CAP1000** (China's standardized version of the AP1000): about a dozen.
- **APR1400**: four, all in South Korea.
- **Everything else**: two EPRs (UK), two ABWRs (Japan, suspended), fast reactors (China's CFR-600, Russia's BREST-OD-300, India's PFBR) and small reactors (Canada's BWRX-300, China's ACP100, Russia's RITM-200S, Argentina's CAREM).

By type, pressurized water reactors dominate. VVER, Hualong One, CAP1000, [APR1400](/en/posts/apr1400-explained/) and EPR are all PWRs; the boiling water reactors are the two Japanese ABWRs and Canada's BWRX-300. By origin, Russian and Chinese designs make up most of the list.

Small modular reactors barely register. Besides Darlington, the list holds China's Linglong One (ACP100), two RITM-200S units in Russia, and the stalled CAREM. Most SMR projects are still on the lower rungs of the [licensing ladder](/en/posts/smr-global-status-2026/), at design review or construction permit, so they do not show up in this count yet.

## 3. Why the counts differ: 79, 75 or "about 80"

You will see 79, 75 and "about 80" quoted for the same thing, sometimes from the same organization. Having studied nuclear engineering, I find that nearly all of the gap comes from counting rules and update dates rather than disagreement about facts.

- **Reference date**: the WNA country table is dated October 16 and the unit list October 23, while "about 80" in the explainer text is a round figure. In between, units that pour first concrete are added and units that connect to the grid drop out. Mochovce 4, connected on October 15, already shows as zero for Slovakia in the WNA table updated the next day.
- **Net versus gross**: the WNA country table gives operable capacity as net (after the plant's own consumption) but under-construction capacity as gross. The same reactor carries two different megawatt figures, so check the basis before comparing capacity across databases.
- **Start line**: as World Nuclear News explained in a 2012 article on Barakah, pouring the basemat concrete, often called first concrete, is generally recognized as the point at which a reactor is officially under construction, and it cannot happen until a full construction licence is granted. Site clearing, excavation and groundbreaking ceremonies do not count.
- **Scope**: the WNA list keeps six stalled units (CAREM, Angra 3, Ohma 1, Shimane 3, Khmelnitski 3 and 4) and includes small and fast reactors. Count only large commercial units with active work and the number shrinks.

So always quote a count together with its source and date. Mixing two databases to say construction "rose" or "fell" is an easy way to be wrong.

## 4. 2026 milestones and how long construction takes

The milestones that stood out this year:

- **Shin Hanul 4** (South Korea): first concrete for the reactor building on May 29, which World Nuclear News described as the official start of construction. Its twin, Shin Hanul 3, passed the same point on May 20, 2025.
- **Tianwan 7** (China, VVER-1200): grid connection on October 8, a little over a week after reaching a self-sustaining chain reaction (World Nuclear News, October 8).
- **Rooppur 1** (Bangladesh, VVER-1200): first criticality on October 11 (World Nuclear News, October 12). The Bangladesh Atomic Energy Regulatory Authority approved power start-up on October 29; the unit was not yet connected to the grid (The Business Standard, Bangladesh, October 29).
- **Mochovce 4** (Slovakia, 471 MWe): grid connection on October 15, with power being raised in stages (World Nuclear News, October 16).
- **Saeul 3** (South Korea, APR1400): [first criticality](/en/posts/reactor-criticality-explained/) on April 12, then an automatic trip on August 11 and a manual shutdown on September 6 during testing. As of November 1, we found no announcement of commercial operation, and the WNA list of October 23 still shows it under construction.

Construction time is best read unit by unit rather than as a national average. The cases below use start and milestone dates confirmed in primary reporting (as of November 1, 2026).

| Unit | Work started | Milestone reached | Elapsed |
|---|---|---|---|
| Tianwan 7 | May 2021 | Grid, Oct 8, 2026 | About 5 years 5 months |
| Rooppur 1 | Nov 2017 | First criticality, Oct 11, 2026 | About 9 years, ongoing |
| Mochovce 4 | 1986 | Grid, Oct 15, 2026 | About 40 years (stalled from 1992) |
| Shin Hanul 3 | May 2025 | Completion target 2032 | About 7 years planned |

Tianwan 7 and Rooppur 1 are the same VVER-1200 design, yet one took about five and a half years and the other is past nine. A newcomer country has to build its regulator, grid links and operating workforce alongside the plant, and that is likely part of the gap. Design alone does not predict the schedule.

## 5. The view from Korea

Every APR1400 on the world's construction list is in Korea. The WNA list expects Saeul 3 on the grid in 2026 and Saeul 4 in 2027, and Korea Hydro & Nuclear Power (KHNP, the state utility that operates all of Korea's reactors) targets completion of Shin Hanul 3 and 4 in 2032 and 2033 (World Nuclear News, May 29).

Abroad, the picture is thinner than the headlines suggest:

- **Barakah**: the last of the UAE's four APR1400s left the construction count when unit 4 connected to the grid in March 2024, and all four were in [commercial operation](/en/posts/barakah-five-years/) by September 2024.
- **Dukovany**: KHNP signed the contract for Czech units 5 and 6 in June 2025, but construction of unit 5 is targeted for 2029, so they are not yet in any "under construction" table (World Nuclear News). They join only when first concrete is poured.
- **El Dabaa**: Egypt's four units are Russian VVER-1200s, but in August 2022 KHNP signed a contract with Rosatom subsidiary ASE to build the turbine-island buildings and structures, the non-nuclear part of the plant, and supply their equipment (World Nuclear News).

Our [report on Korean nuclear exports](/en/posts/korea-nuclear-exports/) tracks each deal stage by stage. The construction list is a useful corrective: today, all four of Korea's entries are at home.

## 6. How to read the "under construction" number

The count is a register of reactors that have left the start line, not a timetable of arrivals. Rooppur 1, started in 2017, and Shin Hanul 4, started this year, each count as one. A rising total can mean more starts, or slower finishes.

Suspended units call for the same care. Ohma 1 and Khmelnitski 3 and 4 still count as under construction, yet the WNA list shows them as "suspended" instead of giving an expected start year. Mochovce 4 shows that a stalled unit can finish decades later, so simply deleting them is not right either.

When I look at this statistic, I watch two flows rather than the total: how many units connected to the grid this year, and how many poured first concrete. Only both together tell you whether construction is actually moving.

## Where this goes next

Three things to watch before year-end: Rooppur 1's grid connection, which would bring Bangladesh its first nuclear electricity; Saeul 3's path to commercial operation after two stops in testing; and how many new first-concrete pours China logs in 2026, which will weigh heavily on whether the global count keeps climbing.
