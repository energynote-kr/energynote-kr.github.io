In 1983 the Finnish government wrote down a schedule for its spent nuclear fuel. Pick a repository site by the end of 2000. Start accepting waste around 2020.

On October 22, 2026, 43 years later, Finland filled in nearly the last box on that schedule. The government granted Posiva, the company TVO and Fortum set up to dispose of their spent fuel, an operating licence for its encapsulation plant and the Onkalo underground repository at Olkiluoto, in the municipality of Eurajoki on Finland's west coast. No spent fuel repository had received an operating licence anywhere before.

Some headlines described the repository as already open. It is not, at least not yet. This piece explains what the Onkalo operating licence actually allows, what still has to happen before the first canister goes underground, how the multi-barrier design works, and what the milestone means for South Korea, which has no site at all.

> **Key takeaways**
> - The Finnish government licensed Onkalo on October 22, 2026, through the end of 2070, for up to 6,500 tonnes of uranium in spent fuel, with a periodic safety assessment due within ten years.
> - The licence does not start disposal. STUK, Finland's nuclear regulator, must still inspect the facility and issue a separate decision; as of November 12, 2026, no start date has been announced.
> - South Korea has no repository site. Its draft third basic plan (November 3, 2026) targets a site by 2038, interim storage before 2050 and disposal before 2060.

## 1. What the Onkalo operating licence covers

The licensed facility has two parts. Above ground, an encapsulation plant seals spent fuel into disposal canisters. Below it, Onkalo holds the disposal tunnels, 400 to 450 meters down in bedrock, according to STUK and World Nuclear News.

The main licence terms, as set out in the government decision and reported by World Nuclear News (October 23, 2026) and NEI Magazine (October 26, 2026):

- **Term**: valid until the end of 2070.
- **Spent fuel cap**: up to 6,500 tonnes of uranium (tU) from the Olkiluoto and Loviisa nuclear plants. That is about 3,300 canisters, disposed of at no more than 60 a year once operations are established, with disposal expected to run into the 2120s.
- **Other waste**: up to 3,000 cubic meters of low- and intermediate-level waste, of which no more than 1,500 may go to final disposal, plus limited amounts of some medical and research waste.
- **Review**: within ten years of the grant, Posiva must submit a periodic safety assessment and an updated long-term safety case to STUK.

The licence rests on the safety statement STUK issued on August 4, 2026. STUK found no obstacle on radiation and nuclear safety grounds to licensing the facility through 2070. Some reports gave the date as August 10; that is the date of the American Nuclear Society's news article, not of STUK's statement.

"World's first" needs a precise reading. Deep geological disposal itself is not new. The US Waste Isolation Pilot Plant (WIPP) in New Mexico has taken defense transuranic waste into a salt formation about 655 meters (2,150 feet) underground since March 1999. What is new is a licence to operate a **repository for spent fuel from power reactors**.

## 2. When will Onkalo start operating?

Short answer: not yet, and nobody has set a date.

Under Finland's Nuclear Energy Act, STUK must inspect the facility before it starts up. Posiva may not begin operation until STUK confirms, in a separate decision, that every requirement for starting operation is met. Posiva says it is still finishing commissioning tests and inspections that STUK has to verify, and it has not published a start-up schedule (as of November 12, 2026).

The trial history so far:

- **August 2024**: Posiva began its trial run of final disposal (TRFD), using canisters loaded with non-radioactive copies of fuel assemblies.
- **Early 2025**: the encapsulation-plant part of the trial was completed, with the last canister moved to underground storage at 430 meters (World Nuclear News, March 5, 2025).
- **Late August 2026**: drilling of the first real deposition hole began (World Nuclear News, October 23, 2026).

We could not confirm from public sources whether the underground part of the trial, which tests the transfer and installation equipment, has been fully completed. Posiva has said it aims to be ready to begin disposal during 2026. The regulator's final inspection will decide when that actually happens.

## 3. How the KBS-3 multi-barrier system works

Onkalo uses KBS-3, a Swedish concept that Posiva and SKB have developed together. The idea is simple. No single barrier has to work alone. From the inside out:

![KBS-3 multi-barrier concept with a cast-iron insert, a 5 cm copper shell, a bentonite buffer and crystalline bedrock 400 to 450 meters deep, next to two timelines: Finland from the 1983 schedule to the 2026 licence and pending STUK inspection, and Korea from a 2027 call for sites to disposal before 2060](/images/onkalo-operating-licence-en.svg)

- **Cast-iron insert**: the load-bearing frame that holds the fuel assemblies in place. A canister for Olkiluoto 1 and 2 fuel holds 12 assemblies, according to Posiva.
- **Copper shell**: 5 centimeters thick, 1.05 meters in diameter and 3.6 to 5.2 meters long depending on the fuel type. A copper lid is welded on to keep groundwater out.
- **Bentonite buffer**: compressed clay blocks. Bentonite swells when wet, filling the space between canister and rock. It cushions small rock movements and, if a canister ever fails, limits and slows the release of radionuclides.
- **Bedrock**: Olkiluoto's ancient crystalline rock. It separates the waste from people and the surface environment and keeps the chemical and groundwater conditions the engineered barriers rely on stable.

### Why copper, and what critics say

Copper is thermodynamically stable in water that contains no oxygen. Groundwater hundreds of meters down holds almost none. That is the core of the design.

There is a known weak point. Sulfide does corrode copper. The Swedish waste company SKB argues that sulfide reaches the canister so slowly that corrosion after 100,000 years would be under 5 millimeters. A research group at Sweden's Royal Institute of Technology (KTH) has challenged the premise that copper is stable in oxygen-free water, and an external review published by the Swedish Radiation Safety Authority (SSM) called SKB's corrosion model too simple.

Having studied nuclear engineering and radiation science, I read that debate less as a flaw than as the reason the clay and the rock are there. The design does not ask copper to carry 100,000 years on its own.

## 4. Why 100,000 years? Decay heat and radioactivity over time

Fresh spent fuel is hot and intensely radioactive. Posiva's figures: its radioactivity falls to about one-hundredth of the original level within a year, and to about one-thousandth by the time the fuel is disposed of after roughly 40 years of cooling. The early decades see the steepest drop.

That is why fuel is cooled for decades before disposal. Residual heat can alter bentonite, so a 2003 Posiva thermal design report limits the canister surface to 100°C and uses 90°C as the calculated maximum to leave margin. Canister spacing is set by the same heat budget, continuing the story of decay heat we followed in [our piece on full spent fuel pools](/en/posts/spent-fuel-pool-full-reactor-shutdown/).

The long tail comes from long-lived nuclides. Plutonium-239 alone has a half-life of about 24,000 years. Posiva says the radioactivity of spent fuel takes about 250,000 years to fall to the level of a rich uranium ore deposit.

So "100,000 years" is best read as a design yardstick rather than a physical constant. It is a way of asking whether isolation holds over geological time, including future ice ages. The number itself matters less than the question.

## 5. Finland's 43 years and South Korea's clock

Finland's path, in dates:

- **1983**: government decision sets the schedule.
- **2000–2001**: Eurajoki's council votes 20 to 7 in favor, the government issues a decision in principle at the end of 2000, and parliament ratifies it 159 to 3 in May 2001 (World Nuclear Association).
- **November 2015**: construction licence.
- **December 30, 2021**: operating licence application.
- **August 4 and October 22, 2026**: STUK's safety statement and the government licence.

South Korea has no site. The High-Level Radioactive Waste Management Special Act, passed in 2025, sets target dates of 2050 for an interim storage facility and 2060 for a disposal facility, according to the Ministry of Trade, Industry and Energy (March 18, 2025).

On November 3, 2026, Korea's High-Level Radioactive Waste Management Committee, a government committee set up under the 2025 special act, released the draft third basic plan, covering 2027 to 2056, with the same targets. According to Korean outlets Electronic Times and Money Today Broadcasting, the draft calls for a call for municipal applicants in 2027, basic surveys in 2028–2033, detailed surveys by 2037 and a site decision by 2038, with disposal about 500 meters underground. Public comment runs to November 23.

Finland took 26 years just to get from site selection to an operating licence, and its first disposal is still ahead. Korea's draft allows 22 years from a 2038 site decision to disposal before 2060. Site-by-site storage pressure is in [our report on Korea's spent fuel](/en/posts/korea-spent-fuel-dry-storage/), and other countries' attempts in [our survey of the world's repositories](/en/posts/world-repositories/).

## Where this goes next

The next dated marker for Onkalo is STUK's final inspection and its decision to allow operation, followed by the first canister of real spent fuel going underground. Neither has a published date as of November 12, 2026.

Finland did not keep every date it set in 1983. What it did do, after local consent and the 2000 site choice, was never reopen the question of where. Remembering how long Korea's [Gyeongju low- and intermediate-level repository](/en/posts/gyeongju-repository-30-years/) took, the first years after the 2027 call may matter as much as any engineering.
