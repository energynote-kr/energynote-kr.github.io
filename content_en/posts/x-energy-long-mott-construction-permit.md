Seadrift, Texas, is a small town on the Gulf Coast. Next to it sits a Dow chemical complex that turns out more than 4 billion pounds of products a year, the raw material for things like food and medical packaging, according to the US Department of Energy (May 2025).

On November 9, 2026, the US Nuclear Regulatory Commission (NRC) issued a construction permit for four reactors at that site. The design is X-energy's Xe-100, a small modular reactor cooled not by water but by helium gas. The NRC called it the first construction permit for a high-temperature gas-cooled reactor (HTGR) in nearly 60 years.

A permit sounds like a switch being flipped. It is not. This piece separates what the Long Mott construction permit allows from what it does not, explains why an HTGR and its TRISO fuel behave so differently from a conventional reactor, and looks at why a chemical company wants steam more than electricity.

> **Key takeaways**
> - On November 9, 2026, the NRC permitted Dow's subsidiary Long Mott Energy to build four Xe-100 HTGRs (80 MWe / 200 MWt each) in Calhoun County, Texas. It is the first HTGR construction permit in nearly 60 years.
> - The permit covers construction only. Loading fuel and operating need a separate operating license, and Dow's preliminary investment decision is expected in 2027.
> - The fuel is TRISO: poppy-seed-sized particles whose silicon carbide layer is the main barrier to fission products. The Oak Ridge plant that will make it received its NRC license in February 2026 and needs a final NRC inspection before it can receive HALEU.

## 1. What the Long Mott construction permit covers

The permit went to Long Mott Energy, a wholly owned subsidiary of Dow, for the Long Mott Generating Station in Calhoun County, Texas (NRC press release 26-083, November 9, 2026).

- **Size**: four Xe-100 units, each 80 MWe and 200 MWt, or 320 MWe for the plant (ANS Nuclear News, November 10, 2026).
- **Purpose**: mainly high-temperature steam and power for Dow's Seadrift Operations, with surplus electricity sent to the grid (NRC).
- **Funding**: the project is one of the two demonstrations in DOE's Advanced Reactor Demonstration Program (ARDP). On X-energy's second-quarter 2026 earnings call, its chief executive said DOE would provide an additional $1 billion in cost-shared funding; ANS put the combined DOE support for the Seadrift project at roughly $2.15 billion (ANS, August 17, 2026).

The NRC listed several firsts. It is the agency's first approval of a multimodule advanced reactor facility, of a pebble-bed core and of a design that refuels while running. ANS described it as the first NRC construction permit for a reactor on an industrial site.

The review ran on its 18-month clock. Long Mott applied on March 31, 2025, and the NRC notified the company on May 12, 2025 that the application had been accepted for docketing (Federal Register, June 10, 2025). An environmental assessment with a finding of no significant impact followed in May 2026 (NRC), the final safety evaluation on October 30 (World Nuclear News, November 10, 2026), and the permit on November 9.

The NRC called this the third advanced reactor construction permit it has issued in 2026. ANS named the other two: TerraPower's Natrium plant in Wyoming in March, and TVA's BWRX-300 at Clinch River in September.

## 2. Construction permit vs operating license: the Part 50 two-step

Long Mott applied under 10 CFR Part 50 (Federal Register, June 10, 2025). Part 50 licenses a reactor in two steps.

- **Construction permit** (CP): the NRC checks that the site and the preliminary design meet safety requirements and allows the plant to be built. Design details can be finished during construction.
- **Operating license** (OL): applied for separately as the plant nears completion. The NRC reviews the final design, the as-built plant and the operating organization before fuel can be loaded.

The alternative is Part 52, which issues a combined construction and operating license (COL). That route needs a far more complete design up front. Many first-of-a-kind advanced reactors pick Part 50 for that reason.

South Korea also licenses construction and operation separately, so readers who know [Korea's reactor licensing steps](/en/posts/korea-nuclear-licensing-process/) will find the logic familiar.

The NRC stated plainly that the Long Mott permit authorizes building the facility, not operating it. The business clock is early too. ANS reports that Dow has not made a final investment decision; a preliminary one is expected in 2027. If approved, construction could start later this decade, with commercial operation in the early 2030s (as reported November 10, 2026).

## 3. How an HTGR differs from a pressurized water reactor

Most of the world's reactors are light-water reactors, where water both cools the core and slows down neutrons. The Xe-100 gives those two jobs to different materials.

As of November 2026, per X-energy's specifications and ANS (September 18, 2026):

| Feature | PWR | Xe-100 HTGR |
|---|---|---|
| Coolant | Water, high pressure | Helium gas |
| Moderator | Water | Graphite |
| Fuel form | Fuel rod assemblies | 6 cm graphite pebbles |
| Outlet temp. | Around 320°C | 750°C |
| Refueling | Shut down to refuel | Pebbles cycle during operation |

About 220,000 pebbles circulate through an Xe-100 core, according to ANS. DOE says each pebble spends a little over three years in the reactor and passes through the core up to six times. Pebbles go in at the top and come out at the bottom, so there is no refueling outage.

Helium absorbs almost no neutrons and so barely becomes radioactive. It does not boil, which lets the core run much hotter. World Nuclear News reports that the design is expected to support an emergency planning zone of about 400 meters, against 10 miles (16 km) for conventional light-water reactors.

The US has built an HTGR before. Fort St. Vrain in Colorado (330 MWe) started construction in September 1968, entered commercial operation in 1979 and shut down for good on August 29, 1989 (World Nuclear Association reactor database). The NRC's "nearly 60 years" lines up with the gap since that 1968 start.

Elsewhere, HTGRs already run. China put its HTR-PM into commercial operation in December 2023, as tracked in [our 2026 SMR licensing map](/en/posts/smr-global-status-2026/).

## 4. TRISO fuel: a containment vessel in every particle

The Xe-100's safety case starts with a grain of fuel. A TRISO particle is a uranium kernel wrapped in layers of carbon and ceramic, about the size of a poppy seed (DOE).

![Cross-section of a TRISO particle with five layers, from fuel kernel to porous carbon buffer, inner pyrocarbon, silicon carbide and outer pyrocarbon, next to the Long Mott licensing timeline from the March 2025 application to the November 9, 2026 permit, a 2027 preliminary investment decision, an undated operating license and target startup in the early 2030s](/images/long-mott-xe100-en.svg)

- **Fuel kernel**: uranium, carbon and oxygen. The Xe-100 uses uranium enriched to 15.5% U-235 (DOE).
- **Porous carbon buffer**: leaves space for fission gases, lowering the pressure on the shells.
- **Inner pyrocarbon** (IPyC): a dense carbon layer that supports the silicon carbide.
- **Silicon carbide** (SiC): the main barrier against metallic and gaseous fission products (Oak Ridge National Laboratory).
- **Outer pyrocarbon** (OPyC): protects the particle and bonds it to the graphite matrix.

Thousands of these particles are pressed into each pebble. DOE says improved TRISO fuel reached 19% peak burnup in a three-year irradiation, then spent more than 300 hours at up to 1,800°C. The particles showed no to minimal damage, under conditions beyond the predicted worst-case HTGR accident.

Having studied nuclear engineering and radiation science, I read this as moving the barrier rather than removing one. A light-water reactor stacks cladding, vessel and containment from the inside out; TRISO splits the innermost barrier into billions of tiny shells. That only holds if manufacturing quality holds, which is why verifying production fuel belongs to the operating license stage.

### Fuel supply is still being built

The Xe-100 needs [HALEU, uranium enriched to between 5% and 20%](/en/posts/uranium-enrichment-explained/), which is not yet available in bulk in the US. X-energy has long-term HALEU enrichment services agreements with Centrus Energy (ANS, August 17, 2026).

X-energy's fuel subsidiary TRISO-X received an NRC Part 70 special nuclear material license, with an initial 40-year term, in February 2026 for its TX-1 and TX-2 plants in Oak Ridge, Tennessee. At that point TX-1 had been under construction since November 2025. The NRC must complete a final inspection before it can receive HALEU (X-energy, February 13, 2026; ANS, February 17, 2026).

## 5. Why a chemical company wants steam, not just electricity

One Xe-100 turns 200 MW of heat into 80 MW of electricity. By simple division that is roughly 40% conversion; the other 60% or so leaves as waste heat.

Using the heat directly avoids that loss. Chemical plants burn through high-temperature steam to heat reactors and run distillation columns, and X-energy says the Xe-100's steam generator delivers steam at about 565°C, much hotter than a light-water reactor can offer.

For Dow, the driver is replacement. DOE says the reactors would replace aging power and steam assets at Seadrift and that the project would be the first advanced nuclear facility at a US industrial site.

The counterarguments are serious. We could find no published total cost for this first-of-a-kind plant (as of November 14, 2026). Dow and X-energy say no final decision has been made, and Dow says several important milestones remain before construction could begin (ANS, November 10, 2026). It is also a demonstration backed by federal cost share. Whether a similar plant would make the same choice without that support is an open question.

## 6. Where South Korea fits

No Korean company has been named as builder of Long Mott (as of November 14, 2026). On equipment, Doosan Enerbility is already tied to the first project, and Korea has several other links to X-energy.

- **Doosan Enerbility** (Korea's reactor-vessel manufacturer): has worked with X-energy since 2021 on making Xe-100 components easier to manufacture. In December 2025 it signed a binding reservation agreement to make reactor pressure vessels, steam generator pressure vessels and other main power system steel for 16 Xe-100 units, and committed to a new SMR fabrication facility in Changwon sized for about 20 reactors a year (X-energy, December 18, 2025). X-energy said the 16 units cover its first two commercial projects; Korean coverage listed the first projects as Dow's four units in Texas and Energy Northwest's 12 in Washington State (Etoday, December 12, 2025). A reservation is not a purchase order.
- **Doosan Enerbility, steam generator tubing**: on November 4, 2026, X-energy announced the first order of steam generator tubing for the proposed four-unit Dow plant in Texas. Sweden's Alleima makes the tubing and Doosan bends it into helical coils (X-energy, November 4, 2026).
- **KHNP** (Korea Hydro & Nuclear Power, the state utility that operates Korea's commercial reactors): announced a partnership with X-energy, Amazon and Doosan in August 2025, during President Lee Jae Myung's visit to the US. The Korean side agreed to support Amazon and X-energy's plan for more than 5 GW of new US nuclear capacity by 2039 (X-energy, August 25, 2025).
- **DL E&C** (a Korean construction company): bought KRW 25 billion (about $20 million) of X-energy convertible bonds in January 2023, with Doosan Enerbility also taking part, according to Korean daily Etoday (January 18, 2023). X-energy put the two companies' combined investment at $25 million (X-energy, January 17, 2023).
- **KAERI** (Korea Atomic Energy Research Institute): reported in a 2015 paper that it had demonstrated at laboratory scale core technologies for producing hydrogen with heat from a very high temperature gas-cooled reactor, including the sulfur-iodine water-splitting process and TRISO fuel fabrication.

What separates a contract from an intention is usually the label on the paper; [our map of Korean builders in Western SMRs](/en/posts/korean-builders-western-smrs/) sorts those labels project by project.

## Where this goes next

Three things have to line up before Long Mott produces steam: Dow's investment decisions, the TRISO-X fuel plant starting up, and an operating license. None has a firm date as of November 14, 2026.

The permit still changes something. A US licensing system built around water-cooled reactors has reviewed a helium-cooled, graphite-moderated, pebble-fueled design on schedule. For Korea, which has studied HTGR hydrogen for years, it is worth watching whether this becomes the first commercial case of nuclear heat run straight into an industrial plant.

Companies named here: X-energy (Nasdaq: XE, listed April 2026) designs the reactor and makes its fuel; Dow (NYSE: DOW) is the customer; Doosan Enerbility (KRX: 034020) holds the component reservation and coils the steam generator tubing; DL E&C (KRX: 375500) is an early investor; Centrus Energy (NYSE American: LEU) has long-term HALEU enrichment agreements with X-energy. Most of these ties are reservations, partnership announcements or equity stakes, not firm orders.
